# FINDINGS - seat 29 (area 9 SPEED), only ever added

## THE FIRST RULE (29-N19): a zero must always be a measurement, never a default
If a harness cannot find the line, the field or the token it was told to read, it must ABORT. It must never fall
back to 0, to an empty string, or to "no difference". A defaulted zero is indistinguishable from a measured zero in
the output, and it will one night confirm the exact claim you are testing.
Worked cases, all mine, all caught by this rule:
- counters placed above a `template` line gave a binary that recorded **0 probes**; without the guard it would have
  printed "0.0 % of probes reach the scan" — a spectacular false confirmation of the claim under test;
- `callgrind_annotate --auto=yes` repeats every line across sections, so an attributed sum came out 13.6 B against a
  3.5 B PROGRAM TOTALS; the harness now aborts when PROGRAM TOTALS is absent;
- a share computed against the sum of the rows instead of PROGRAM TOTALS inflated every percentage ~4x;
- a `git checkout` mid-build reverted half an edit and the counters stopped reporting: abort, not zeros.
Implementation: assert the anchor exists before patching it; assert the parsed field exists before using it; assert
the denominator is the program total, not the subset; print "NOT FOUND" and a verdict of INCONCLUSIVE when a symbol
is missing, never 0.

### The same rule one level up (29-N27)
**When a tally's denominator is smaller than the number of rows visible in the raw file, that is a parse bug, not a
result.** A silently-dropped row is as persuasive as a defaulted zero: my own move-quality tally matched only `cp`
scores, silently discarded 4 of 17 rows (all mate judgements), and reported "5 PC20 / 7 S14" when the full data was
**7-7-3**. Requiring every row to parse, and ABORTING with the unreadable rows listed, is the only safe form.



### The failure mode, named precisely (29-N33)
**Writing the caveat and then contradicting it in the same row.** A row that says "2-2-1 over five disagreements"
and "dead heat on both sets" in one sentence has already disarmed itself: the caveat is present, so it reads as
careful, and the claim is still the strong one. Three instances tonight, each caught only by re-reading for the WORD
rather than the number:
- 29-F31: "the inlined copy is more expensive per call" (my own disassembly refuted it);
- 29-F44: "4 of 6 favouring PC20" read as PC20 choosing better moves;
- 29-F49: "dead heat on both … and late G (2-2-1 over 5)".
Underlying cause in all three: a MEASUREMENT statement and a GENERALISATION welded into one sentence. The fix is
mechanical — let the weak sample keep the weaker word ("no sign of X", not "X"), and put the generalisation in a
separate sentence that names its scope.

## NAMED RULE (29-N23): sweep the second variable before any number reaches a summary
Twice in one hour a single-cell percentage of mine was wrong the same way, and both times the fix was to vary one
more axis:
- replacement-scan rate: first measured **11.1 %** at one depth/hash, actually **0.1 % to 44.8 %** across the 2x2
  (29-F38 -> 29-F39);
- PC20 tree size: first measured **-19.5 %** nodes at depth 12, actually **+2.3 % at depth 10**, -19.5 % at 12,
  -14.1 % at 14 — the switches are NOT free at low depth (29-F40 -> 29-F41).
Practice: before a figure enters a finding, a rule or a summary, measure it at another value of whatever it most
plausibly depends on (depth, hash, position set, settings regime, sample size). If the answer moves, report the TABLE,
not the number. A single figure may stand only when the sweep shows it is flat — and then the flatness is the finding.


### The sample-size worked example (29-F51 / 29-N35), beside the sweep rule
**Any tally under about twenty outcomes is a hint at most.** Four samples of the SAME measurement — S14-vs-PC20
disagreements judged under a neutral regime — gave four different directions:

| sample | outcomes | PC20 | S14 | tie | apparent story |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R_2911 depth 12 | 17 | 7 | 7 | 3 | dead heat |
| late G depth 10 | 5 | 2 | 2 | 1 | dead heat |
| R_2911 depth 14 (30 pos) | 3 | **0** | **3** | 0 | "PC20 is worse at depth 14" — published as 29-F50, then WITHDRAWN |
| R_2911 depth 14 (80 pos) | 13 | **8** | **4** | 1 | mildly in PC20's favour |

The 3-outcome sample produced a confident-looking 3-0 the wrong way; the 13-outcome sample of the same configuration
reversed it. That is the argument for the threshold, and it is why 29-F50 lasted twenty minutes before 29-F51
overturned it. Treat a small one-sided tally as a reason to collect more, never as a finding.

## NAMED RULE (29-N16): an instruction-count saving on one build is NOT evidence about another build
Worked example, my own TT patch, same two source changes:
- base tree 6b98f217:            -18,708,563 instructions  (-0.414 %)
- PC r15 build, PC20:            +11,318,305 instructions  (+0.320 %)
- and on the PC build each half alone is worse: slot-0 +0.558 %, vector gather +0.634 %
Node counts are identical in all four PC builds and the two `probe_body` bodies are byte-identical between the two
trees, so the swing is compiler outlining/layout, not logic. The effect is also NON-ADDITIVE on the PC build: each
half alone is worse than base (+0.558 %, +0.634 %) while together they are +0.320 %, a -0.872 % interaction term.
NOTE: my first explanation ("the inlined copy is more expensive per call") was REFUTED by disassembly — the patched
probe body is LARGER (119 -> 140 instructions), and what actually collapsed was `probe_owned` (116 -> 18) as its
callers absorbed it. See 29-F31 / 29-N17.
CONSEQUENCE: re-measure on the build you intend to recommend, every time. Evidence: `pc0/raw_factorial.txt`.

## NAMED RULE (29-F35): `bench` is not a representative workload for a slot-sensitive optimisation
Same build, same instrumentation, two workloads:
- `bench 13`            : 203,057 TT hits, slot0 **96.0 %** (3.8 / 0.2 / 0.0 %), generation writes **0**
- real games, depth 12  : 6,084,620 TT hits, **26.3 / 25.3 / 24.7 / 23.7 %**, generation writes **16,811**
A change whose benefit scales with slot-0 hits therefore looks ~**3.7x** better under `bench` purely from workload
shape, and bench never exercises the age/depth replacement path at all.
CONSEQUENCE: any transposition-table optimisation must be measured on real-game positions, never on `bench`.
This one applies past my patch. Evidence: `slots/raw_slot_regimes.txt`.
SECOND BENCH OBJECTION, same conclusion by a different route: bench also cannot measure the table's COST or its
replacement scan at all. Measured myself (29-C8, own instrumentation, PC r15 build, Threads 1, Hash 16): **bench 13
makes 861,904 probes, 576,467 of which enter the replacement scan, but 576,417 of those find an empty slot — leaving
exactly 50 (0.006 %) that perform an age/depth comparison.** On real games the same scan reaches an age/depth
comparison far more often, and the exact share depends on DEPTH and HASH together — 6.3 % (Hash 16, depth 10),
44.8 % (Hash 16, depth 12), 0.1 % (Hash 64, depth 10) and 2.4 % (Hash 64, depth 12) on one instrumented build
(29-F39); probe counts are identical across Hash sizes at a given depth, so the table size changes only how often a
scan finds an EMPTY slot, i.e. whether the table is saturated. So this rule needs NO specific percentage: the
robust statement is the **0.006 % on bench against a much higher rate in real play at a saturated table**. Seat 19's
19-F18 reports the same 50 on bench and 54.7 % on their set, which is within the range my sweep shows, so their
figure is consistent with mine once the workload and saturation are named. Together the two objections mean bench is the wrong instrument for
transposition-table work on BOTH grounds: the distribution it samples and the code paths it never enters.
