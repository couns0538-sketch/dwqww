The mid-game stop can also survive the WORKER itself dying. I repeated the same genuine native game and unmodified wrapper with a separate parent supervisor. It started only its own worker session, checked that the engine belonged to that same session and process group, and held a live process handle. After observing actual search CPU, I killed the worker process but initially left its engine alive.

In all six runs the orphaned native engine gained two further CPU ticks in the next 24 ms. In the three controls, killing the worker alone left the game consuming three additional native CPU ticks in the 30 ms AFTER D. In the three guarded runs the surviving supervisor stopped the owned group, verified native death 14.67 / 14.52 / 14.64 ms before D and reaped the child. These are deliberately shortened Linux test clocks; the day needs a margin measured under its actual load.

This separates two requirements. The live worker can record the interrupted ID and reason when only its engine is retired. If the worker has already died, the external supervisor still enforces D; the finalizer reports the ID from the durable evidence it actually has, including dispatch_unknown when appropriate. A supervisor inside the same worker cannot cover the second case by itself. This gives the PC a reproducible reason to keep the guard's independent lifetime in the paid-path acceptance test.
